A plain-text novel and a 40-page illustrated children's book are not the same formatting job, and quoting one flat number for "ebook formatting" hides more than it reveals. The honest answer is a range that depends on what you're formatting, who does it, and how many places it needs to work.

## The short answer

| Route | Typical cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DIY software | $0 – $250 | Plain-text manuscripts, one retailer, willing to learn a tool |
| Freelancer (Upwork, Fiverr, Reedsy) | $50 – $400 | Straightforward fiction/nonfiction, tighter budgets |
| Professional formatting service | $300 – $1,500+ | Multiple retailers, print + ebook, revision support |
| Custom / illustrated formatting | $1,500 – $5,000+ | Children's books, cookbooks, heavily designed nonfiction |

These are market ranges gathered from formatting services, marketplaces and publishing guides, not a single vendor's price list — treat the low and high ends as real but not universal, since your specific manuscript can land outside them.

## DIY software: $0-250

The free tier is real but limited. Calibre and Amazon's own Kindle Create convert a manuscript into an ebook file at no cost, but the output is plain and the tools are unforgiving of anything beyond simple chapter breaks.

The paid tier is where most self-publishers who go the DIY route actually land. Atticus is a one-time purchase around $147 and runs on any operating system; Vellum is around $250 and is Mac-only, according to [The Books Central's formatting cost breakdown](https://thebookscentral.com/blog/book-formatting-cost/).

Both produce clean, professional-looking output for straightforward text, and because they're one-time purchases rather than subscriptions, the cost is fixed no matter how many books you format afterward — which is the same "own it once" logic that a tool like Cambric's $199 one-time license leans on in its own [book formatting cost guide](https://cambric.pub/guides/how-much-does-book-formatting-cost/).

Where DIY software runs into trouble: illustrated layouts, complex footnotes, multiple embedded fonts, and formatting for several retailers at once with each one's quirks. That's usually the point where a plain-text novel stays DIY and an illustrated book doesn't.

## Freelancers: $50-400

Hiring an individual formatter through Upwork, Fiverr, or Reedsy is the middle path — more polish and troubleshooting than software alone, less overhead than an agency.

Upwork's own project-pricing guidance for eBook specialists breaks down by scope: basic manuscript conversion runs roughly $200-$500 per project, layout formatting $500-$1,000, and more complex work like multi-format export or validation and repair climbing into the $1,000-$3,500+ range for senior-level freelancers, per [Upwork's eBooks freelancer marketplace](https://www.upwork.com/hire/ebooks-freelancers/).

At the lower end, individual freelancers on Fiverr commonly price a standard-length ebook formatting job well under $200, and [Kindlepreneur's roundup of formatting services](https://kindlepreneur.com/book-formatting-services/) cites Reedsy freelance formatters at roughly $200-$300 for a standard-length fiction book.

The wide spread reflects experience level and how the freelancer prices scope creep. A $50 gig on Fiverr for basic conversion and a $400 Reedsy formatter handling a full print-and-ebook package are both accurately described as "freelance formatting" — read the gig scope carefully before comparing two quotes as if they're the same job.

## Professional formatting services: $300-1,500+

Dedicated formatting services and small studios sit above individual freelancers in price and typically include more structured process: a style sheet, a proof round, and support across multiple output formats.

Kindlepreneur's comparison of formatting services lists concrete anchors: Ebook Launch starts at $219 for books up to 50,000 words, Elite Authors starts at $329 per book, and BookBaby starts at $399, with most professional services clustering in the $200-$400 range for a standard book (per [Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/book-formatting-services/)).

The Books Central's own breakdown gives a similar shape by category: basic ebook formatting at $50-$150, print-and-ebook combined at $200-$500, and academic or technical books — which need footnotes, indexes, and consistent cross-references — at $400-$1,000+ (per [The Books Central](https://thebookscentral.com/blog/book-formatting-cost/)).

What pushes a project past the $500-$700 mark within this tier is usually one of: a second or third retailer format, a print interior in addition to the ebook, multiple revision rounds, or a rush turnaround — The Books Central notes rush fees commonly add 25-50% on top of the base rate for a 48-hour turnaround versus the standard seven-to-fourteen-day window.

## Custom and illustrated formatting: $1,500-5,000+

Children's books, cookbooks, heavily designed nonfiction, and anything with fixed-layout image placement is a fundamentally different job from reflowable text formatting, and the price reflects that.

The Books Central puts children's and illustrated books at $300-$800 for simpler projects, but that's a floor — full custom illustrated ebooks with original layout design, image licensing coordination, and fixed-layout EPUB work (which behaves more like print design than text formatting) regularly run into the $1,500-$5,000+ range once you account for a designer's time on each spread rather than a template applied to flowing text.

If your manuscript falls in this category, get a scoped quote against your actual page count and illustration count rather than budgeting from a text-formatting range — the two aren't comparable per-page.

A 32-page picture book with full-bleed illustrations on every spread and a 300-page illustrated cookbook with recipe photos every few pages both fall under "custom/illustrated," but the second has roughly ten times the page count doing similar layout work, and a fair quote should reflect that rather than applying one flat "illustrated" rate to both.

## The hidden costs nobody quotes upfront

A formatting quote rarely mentions three things that can change the real cost of the project:

- **Revisions.** Ask how many rounds of proof corrections are included before extra ones are billed separately. A $300 quote with one included revision and a $50 fee per additional round can end up costing more than a $400 quote with three rounds built in.
- **Who owns the source files.** Some formatters hand over the editable source (InDesign file, Vellum project, etc.) when the job is done; others only deliver the final EPUB/MOBI and keep the source file themselves. If you'll need to update the book later — a typo fix, a new edition, an added chapter — not owning the source means paying the same formatter (or a new one, from scratch) for every future change.
- **Reformatting for a second platform.** A file built and validated for Amazon KDP doesn't automatically pass Apple Books' or Kobo's own validation checks — each platform has different formatting requirements and metadata fields. If you plan to distribute beyond a single retailer, confirm upfront whether multi-platform formatting is included or billed as a separate pass, since it's a common source of a "final" invoice that's larger than the original quote.

Ask about all three before you accept a quote, not after the invoice arrives.

## DIY vs. pay: a simple decision guide

Work through these in order — the first "no" tells you which route fits:

1. **Is your manuscript plain text with no illustrations or complex layout?** If no, skip DIY software and go straight to a formatter or service that handles illustrated/fixed-layout work.
2. **Are you formatting for just one retailer?** If no, weigh whether a DIY tool's multi-format export is reliable enough for your needs, or whether a service's platform-specific validation is worth paying for.
3. **Do you have a few evenings to learn a formatting tool, and does your timeline allow for a learning curve?** If no, hiring saves you the time cost even if the software would technically work.
4. **Is this a one-off project, or will you be formatting multiple books over time?** If it's ongoing, a one-time DIY tool purchase (Atticus, Vellum) amortizes across every future book in a way that per-project freelancer or service fees don't.
5. **Does the project need a genuinely designed layout — illustrations, a cookbook grid, a fixed-layout children's book?** If yes, that's custom/illustrated territory regardless of your answers above, and it's worth budgeting for a designer rather than forcing it through general-purpose formatting software.

## What a formatting quote should actually spell out

A vague quote is the most common source of surprise costs. Whether you're comparing a freelancer, a service, or deciding DIY software is enough, ask each quote to answer the same five questions before you accept it:

- **Which output formats are included?** EPUB and MOBI/AZW3 for Kindle are common defaults, but PDF for print or a fixed-layout format for illustrated work is often a separate line item, not an assumption.
- **How many revision rounds are built in?** Covered above, but worth restating as the single biggest source of quote-vs-invoice mismatch.
- **Who owns the source file when the project ends?** Also covered above — get this in writing, not implied.
- **What's the turnaround, and what does rushing it cost?** The Books Central's research puts standard turnaround at seven to fourteen days, with 25-50% rush premiums for 48-hour delivery — know which one you're actually quoted for.
- **Does the price cover formatting only, or does it include proofing for formatting-introduced errors** (a stray page break, a dropped italics tag)? Some services proof their own output as standard; others hand back the file and consider the job done the moment it validates against the retailer's checker.

A quote that answers all five upfront is usually more trustworthy than the cheapest number on the page, even before you compare totals.

## DIY software: the two tools compared

Since Atticus and Vellum are the two paid tools that come up most often in the research above, it's worth being specific about what separates them rather than treating "DIY software" as one option:

| | Atticus (~$147) | Vellum (~$250) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Platform | Windows, Mac, Chromebook (browser-based) | Mac only |
| License | One-time, unlimited books | One-time, unlimited books |
| Print layout | Included | Included (separate "Vellum Press" add-on for some features) |
| Learning curve | Moderate | Moderate, often cited as more visual/intuitive |

Both are one-time purchases rather than subscriptions, which is the detail that matters most if you're planning to publish more than one book — the cost amortizes to near zero on your second and third title, unlike paying a freelancer or service per project each time.

## Print formatting: a related but separate cost

If you also want a print edition, don't assume the ebook formatting price covers it. As ByteAndBook's own [eBook & Digital Publishing service page](/services/ebook-publishing/) notes, print and reflowable ebook layout follow different rules — a print interior needs trim size, margins and running heads set for a physical page, which reflowable ebook text doesn't have.

The Books Central's figures reflect this split directly: basic ebook-only formatting runs $50-$150, while print-and-ebook combined climbs to $200-$500 — budget for both if you need both, rather than assuming one quote covers the other.
